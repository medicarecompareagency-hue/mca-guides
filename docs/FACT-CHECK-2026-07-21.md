## The two real problems

### 1. Enrollment Periods — the MA Open Enrollment claim was wrong

The article said the January–March Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period
was only for people already in an MA plan on January 1. That's false. CMS
Product No. 11219 (March 2026) defines a second version for people new to
Medicare, running from the first month they have both Part A and Part B
through the last day of the third month.

As written, an agent following the article would have turned away an
eligible beneficiary. **Fixed**, and the General Enrollment Period — missing
entirely from an article titled "Key 2026 Dates" — was added alongside it.

### 2. Working Past 65 — the HSA omission had tax consequences

The article correctly said Medicare enrollment ends HSA eligibility, but
omitted that Part A is backdated up to six months when you enroll after 65.
The IRS treats that retroactive period as Medicare coverage, so contributions
made during it become excess contributions — income tax plus a 6% excise tax
per year until corrected.

A reader contributing right up to the month they filed could have been hit
with a tax bill created by following this article. **Fixed**, with the
practical rule stated plainly: stop contributing six months before applying.

Also added to that article: COBRA does not create or extend the Part B
Special Enrollment Period (a lifetime-penalty trap), the SEP clock runs from
employment end *or* coverage end whichever comes first, and the Part D
63-day creditable coverage window.

### Also fixed: the SEP duration generalization

The enrollment article said Special Enrollment Periods "generally last about
two months." True for the most common cases, wrong in the ways that matter
most: dual eligibles and Extra Help recipients get a *monthly* window, and
the Part B SEP after employer coverage is *eight months*. Replaced with a
table.

## Still needs a human

**Call Mississippi Division of Medicaid at 800-421-2408** to confirm the MSP
dollar figures ($1,380 / $1,646 / $1,846). Mississippi does not publish MSP
dollar limits publicly — its pages give only FPL percentages. The numbers
come from NCOA's March 2026 chart, which carries its own "verify with your
state agency" disclaimer.

The no-asset-test half of the Mississippi claim *is* primary-sourced (state
Eligibility Policy Manual, Chapter 400) and needs no further checking.

**Note for future editors: the Mississippi figures look wrong and aren't.**
They're higher than federal because Mississippi uses a $50 income disregard
where most states use $20. A well-meaning edit "correcting" them to the
federal numbers would wrongly turn away eligible Mississippians. A footnote
now says so in the article.

Two softer gaps: Alabama's no-asset-test status is confirmed by NCOA and the
Medicare Rights Center but not read directly off an Alabama Medicaid page
(their PDF wouldn't load, and their public flyer is dated February 2025).
Florida and Georgia were confirmed as "federal limits" via NCOA only — low
risk, since their figures match the federal standard exactly.
