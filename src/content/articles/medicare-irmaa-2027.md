If your income is above a certain level, you pay more for Medicare Part B and Part D. For 2027, that starts when your modified adjusted gross income is over $112,000 if you file taxes as a single person, or over $224,000 if you file jointly, according to CMS (cms.gov). Above those lines, your Part B premium goes from the standard $209.40 a month to anywhere from $293.10 to $711.80 a month per person, and you pay an extra amount in addition to your drug plan premium.

That extra charge is called the income-related monthly adjustment amount, or IRMAA. CMS says it affects about 9% of people with Part B. If you're in that group, the most useful thing to know is that it's based on old income, and you can often get it lowered when your income drops.

## Where the income number comes from

Social Security decides whether you owe IRMAA. It doesn't look at what you earn now. It uses the most recent federal tax return the IRS sends over, which is generally from two years earlier. For 2026 premiums, for example, Social Security generally used tax returns for tax year 2024 (ssa.gov). That same two-year gap means your 2027 premiums are generally based on your 2025 tax return.

The income measure is your modified adjusted gross income, or MAGI. Social Security defines it as your adjusted gross income plus any tax-exempt interest income (ssa.gov). That second part catches people off guard. Interest from municipal bonds isn't taxed, but it still counts here.

## 2027 Part B premiums by income

These are the 2027 monthly Part B amounts CMS published on November 17, 2026. The amount is per person, so a married couple both on Medicare each pays it.

| If you file individually | If you file jointly | Your Part B premium |
|---|---|---|
| $112,000 or less | $224,000 or less | $209.40 |
| Over $112,000 up to $141,000 | Over $224,000 up to $282,000 | $293.10 |
| Over $141,000 up to $177,000 | Over $282,000 up to $354,000 | $418.80 |
| Over $177,000 up to $212,000 | Over $354,000 up to $424,000 | $544.40 |
| Over $212,000 and under $500,000 | Over $424,000 and under $750,000 | $670.00 |
| $500,000 or more | $750,000 or more | $711.80 |

The 2027 Part B deductible is $292 for everyone, no matter your income (CMS, cms.gov).

## 2027 Part D adjustments by income

If you have Medicare drug coverage, you pay an added amount beyond your plan's premium, using the same income levels (CMS, cms.gov):

| If you file individually | If you file jointly | Added to your Part D cost each month |
|---|---|---|
| $112,000 or less | $224,000 or less | $0.00 |
| Over $112,000 up to $141,000 | Over $224,000 up to $282,000 | $15.40 |
| Over $141,000 up to $177,000 | Over $282,000 up to $354,000 | $39.70 |
| Over $177,000 up to $212,000 | Over $354,000 up to $424,000 | $64.00 |
| Over $212,000 and under $500,000 | Over $424,000 and under $750,000 | $88.30 |
| $500,000 or more | $750,000 or more | $96.40 |

The Part D adjustment doesn't go to your drug plan. Social Security says it deducts this amount from your monthly Social Security payment no matter how you normally pay your plan premium. If you don't get Social Security payments, or the amount is more than your payment, you get a separate bill from another federal agency (ssa.gov).

## Married but filing separately

The brackets are much steeper if you're married, lived with your spouse at any time during the year, and filed a separate tax return. For 2027, CMS lists three levels for that situation: $112,000 or less pays the standard $209.40 for Part B; over $112,000 and under $388,000 pays $670.00; and $388,000 or more pays $711.80. The Part D adjustments for those same levels are $0.00, $88.30, and $96.40 (cms.gov).

## What people get wrong about IRMAA

**Thinking a one-dollar difference doesn't matter.** It can. The brackets are cliffs, not slopes. A single person with a MAGI of $112,000 pays $209.40 for Part B in 2027. At $112,001, the premium jumps to $293.10, plus $15.40 for Part D. That's why the year you sell a house, take a large IRA withdrawal, or convert money to a Roth can show up as higher Medicare premiums two years later.

**Assuming Medicare Advantage gets you out of it.** It doesn't. You have to keep paying your Part B premium to stay in a Medicare Advantage plan (Medicare.gov), and that includes any IRMAA on it.

**Paying the higher amount after retiring without asking for a new decision.** This is the big one. Your paychecks stop, but Social Security is still looking at the tax return from when you were working full time. You don't have to wait two years for the system to catch up.

**Forgetting about tax-exempt interest.** If you hold municipal bonds, that interest is added back into your MAGI for IRMAA purposes.

## How to get IRMAA lowered

Social Security will make a new decision if a life-changing event reduced your income. Its list of qualifying events (ssa.gov):

- You married, divorced, or your spouse died
- You or your spouse stopped working or cut back your work hours
- You or your spouse lost income-producing property because of a disaster or other event beyond your control
- An employer's pension plan was scheduled to stop, ended, or was reorganized
- You or your spouse received a settlement from an employer or former employer because of the employer's closure, bankruptcy, or reorganization

To ask, use Form SSA-44, "Medicare Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount - Life-Changing Event." You can upload it online after signing in at ssa.gov, mail or fax it to a Social Security office with your evidence, or call 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778) (ssa.gov).

Social Security needs proof of both the event and the drop in income. Examples it gives include a death certificate or a letter from your employer about your retirement. If you've already filed a federal tax return for the year, you'll need to show your signed copy (ssa.gov).

Two other situations also lead to a new decision. If you filed an amended tax return, call Social Security and send a copy of the amended return along with the IRS acknowledgment receipt. If the IRS sent Social Security wrong information, you must correct it with the IRS first (ssa.gov).

Social Security points out that asking for a new decision because of a life-changing event is not an appeal. If you simply disagree with how your amount was figured, that's when you file an appeal, called a request for reconsideration, online or with Form SSA-561-U2 (ssa.gov).

## A practical tip

If you're planning a large one-time income event, like selling property or a big retirement account withdrawal, it's worth talking with your tax professional about timing. The income shows up on that year's tax return, and that return can set your Medicare premiums two years down the road.

For questions about your premium, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213. For general Medicare questions, call 1-800-MEDICARE or visit Medicare.gov. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) also offers free one-on-one counseling.
