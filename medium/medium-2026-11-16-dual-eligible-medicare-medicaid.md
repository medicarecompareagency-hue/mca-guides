# Can I Have Medicare and Medicaid at the Same Time? How Dual Eligibility Works

Yes. You can have Medicare and Medicaid at the same time, and millions of people do. People who have both are called "dual eligible." Medicare stays your main health coverage, and Medicaid fills in behind it, often paying your premiums and some or all of what Medicare leaves you to pay.

The catch is that "having Medicaid" doesn't mean the same thing for everyone. There are different levels of help, and which one you qualify for decides what gets paid.

## Two programs, two different jobs

Medicare is a federal health insurance program, mostly for people 65 and older and people with certain disabilities. You qualify based on age or disability and your work history, not your income.

Medicaid is run by each state, under federal rules. You qualify based on your income and your savings, and the limits differ from state to state. Because one program is based on age and the other on income, plenty of people qualify for both.

## Who pays first

Medicare.gov is plain about this: when you're dual eligible and you get a Medicare-covered service, Medicare pays first. Medicaid pays last, after Medicare and any other health insurance you have. Your doctor or hospital bills Medicare first, and whatever Medicare doesn't pay, such as a deductible or your share of the bill, then goes to Medicaid, depending on your level of coverage.

## Full Medicaid vs. "partial" help

This is the part most people get wrong. There are two broad groups of dual eligibles.

**Full-benefit dual eligibles** qualify for their state's complete Medicaid benefits. According to Medicare.gov, if you have full Medicaid:

- Your state pays your Medicare Part B premium.
- Depending on your Medicaid level, your state may also pay your Medicare deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments, plus your Part A premium if you owe one.
- You automatically get Extra Help with prescription drug costs.
- Medicaid may cover things Medicare doesn't, such as nursing home care and personal care services.

**Partial-benefit dual eligibles** don't get full Medicaid. CMS describes them as people enrolled in Medicare and a Medicare Savings Program only. A Medicare Savings Program is a state program that helps pay Medicare costs, but it doesn't add Medicaid's other benefits.

## The Medicare Savings Programs

Medicare.gov lists four Medicare Savings Programs. You apply through your state, and some states use higher limits than the federal ones below (2026 federal limits for one person, from Medicare.gov).

| Program | What it pays | Monthly income limit | Resource limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) | Part B premium, Part A premium if owed, plus deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments | $1,350 | $9,950 |
| Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB) | Part B premium only | $1,616 | $9,950 |
| Qualifying Individual (QI) | Part B premium only | $1,816 | $9,950 |
| Qualified Disabled and Working Individuals (QDWI) | Part A premium for certain people with disabilities who went back to work and lost premium-free Part A | — | — |

QI must be reapplied for every year, states approve it first-come, first-served, and it's only for people who don't qualify for other Medicaid coverage.

The Part B premium these programs cover is $202.90 a month in 2026 (Medicare.gov). If that premium normally comes out of your Social Security check, this help means it no longer does. Medicare.gov also says each of the first three programs comes with Extra Help for prescription drugs, which caps what you pay for each covered drug at $12.65 in 2026.

## Your providers can't bill you if you have QMB

If you have QMB, Medicare.gov says Medicare providers aren't allowed to bill you for services and items Medicare covers, including deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments. You may still owe a small Medicaid copayment in some cases. Billing mistakes still happen, so show both your Medicare card and your Medicaid or QMB card every time you get care.

## Prescription drugs and coverage choices

Even with full Medicaid, your prescriptions are covered through Medicare, not Medicaid. Medicare.gov says dual eligibles are automatically enrolled in a Medicare drug plan.

Having both programs doesn't lock you into one kind of plan. You can choose Original Medicare or a Medicare Advantage plan. There are also options built for people with both, including Dual Eligible Special Needs Plans and PACE (Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly).

## Common mistakes

**Assuming you earn too much.** Limits vary by state, and some states let you "spend down," counting medical bills against income that's over the limit. CMS has reported that more than half of people eligible for a Medicare Savings Program aren't enrolled. If you're close, apply and let the state decide.

**Thinking you have to pick one program.** You don't. Medicare stays primary. Medicaid works behind it.

**Worrying about Medicaid work requirements.** Starting January 1, 2027 (earlier in some states), certain adults must meet a monthly work or activity requirement to keep Medicaid. Medicare.gov states that this requirement doesn't apply to people with Medicare.

**Letting coverage lapse at renewal.** Medicaid and the Medicare Savings Programs have to be renewed. Open and answer any mail from your state Medicaid office. A missed renewal can mean your Part B premium suddenly comes out of your check again.

## How to apply

Apply through your state Medicaid office. Medicaid.gov lists every state's program and how to contact it. For unbiased help, you can contact your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP), or call 1-800-MEDICARE.
